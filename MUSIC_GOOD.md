# What makes music sound good: a guide for the engine

By Resonance Music Theory, Oct 25 2026. These are **guides, not rules**: tendencies the engine should lean toward, which ohms, cultures and players can bend. Cheap to compute: decide once per bar or phrase and cache the result; do no per-frame theory work off-screen.

1. **Agree in the moment, move over time.** A jam is a big, living thing. It switches keys, runs through many chords and holds many sounds. What makes it sound good is that the ohms playing *at the same moment* hear each other: they share the beat, and the notes sounding together fit the current chord, or clash on purpose and resolve. Key changes and chord movement travel through the jam (for example, one ohm moves and the others follow within a bar or two, often through a shared pivot chord, which is a chord that belongs to both keys). Different sounds and even different scales can sit together when they're spaced in height and time. Most "bad generative music" comes from ohms ignoring each other, not from too much variety.
2. **Consonance by default.** Notes that fit the current chord sound restful. On strong beats, favour chord tones (the root, the third and the fifth of the chord). Keep the other scale notes for weak beats and passing moments. Non-scale notes stay rare and should resolve to a neighbour right away.
3. **Tension, then release.** Music feels good when it leans away from home and then comes back. Use a few chords in a cycle that ends on its home chord (for example I–IV–V–I, or ii–V–I in jazz). Build up toward the end of a phrase, then land. Landing on the home note at a phrase end is the most satisfying moment, so protect it.
4. **Phrases, not streams.** Group music into 2-, 4- or 8-bar phrases that breathe. A phrase is a short idea with a start, a shape (usually up and then down) and a resting point. Leave a gap at the end of a phrase.
5. **Repetition with variation.** Repeat a motif (a short tune idea) so the ear can follow it, then change one thing each time (rhythm, one note, or its height). Plain repetition is boring, and constant novelty is noise. A good mix is about two parts familiar to one part new.
6. **Smooth voice leading.** When the chord changes, each harmony voice moves to the nearest note of the new chord: a step or a held note, not a leap. Melodies mostly move by step, and an occasional leap is followed by a step back the other way.
7. **Rhythm feel.** Lock everything to a shared pulse, then humanise it. Add small timing drift (a few milliseconds), loudness accents on strong beats, and swing or a culture's groove where it fits. Syncopation (accents off the beat) adds energy, but somebody must keep the beat steady so the off-beats have something to push against.
8. **Interlocking parts.** Parts sound best when they fill each other's gaps: call and response, hocket (two players splitting one line note by note) and kotekan-style interlocking. Give each ohm's rhythm its own slots rather than having everyone play on every beat.
9. **Jobs in the music.** For example, at any moment parts tend to cover different jobs: something low and simple (bass or drone), something keeping time, something holding chords, tunes, and sparse decoration. These aren't fixed roles and there's no lead in a jam (Wilbow, 2:22 AM: 'lead' was fudged). Tunes can overlap, answer each other or take turns, as long as they don't crowd one band.
10. **Space in the mix (frequency).** Parts sound clearer in different height bands. For example, sub-bass is below about 60 Hz, bass about 60–250 Hz, chords about 250–800 Hz, and tunes above that with some room. Two ohms in the same band at the same time clash, so move one up or down an octave or have it rest.
11. **Space in time (density).** Silence is part of the music. Cap the total notes per bar across the jam, so the more ohms join, the less each one plays. A big jam should sound fuller, not busier.
12. **Balance loudness.** For example, tunes are clear, low parts are felt, and decoration is quiet. Scale each ohm by what it's playing and by its instrument's natural loudness, then normalise for jam size, so ten ohms aren't ten times louder than one.
13. **Low notes need wide spacing.** Close intervals (seconds and thirds) sound muddy below about 200 Hz. Down low, use octaves and fifths, and keep the tight chord shapes higher up.
14. **Change at the edges.** Key changes, new chords and ohms joining or leaving tend to land on a bar or phrase boundary, ideally signalled a bar ahead (for example, by whichever ohm starts the change). Changes in the middle of a bar sound like mistakes.
15. **Shape over time.** A jam that builds and relaxes sounds composed. Start sparse, add parts, peak, thin out and end on the home chord. This is the path from jam to song.
16. **Tuning honesty.** Real instruments and traditions can use other tunings (maqam quarter tones, just intervals, inharmonic gamelan). Within one jam, everyone shares the jam's tuning. Mixing tunings unintentionally sounds out of tune, while doing it on purpose is a feature.
17. **Sounds are mostly stable, but they can grow.** An ohm's sound comes from its body, and it generally keeps it, so you can recognise it. It can also learn new sounds from others over time, the player can change it directly, and carried items modulate it in clear, predictable ways. Jamming mostly changes what it plays and how it plays.
